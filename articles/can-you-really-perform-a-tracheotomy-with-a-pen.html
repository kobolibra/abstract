Can you really perform a tracheotomy with a pen?

Yes. But as a new book shows, it has better uses

Oct 1st 2026

It was November 1944. German soldiers were blocking the entranceways to the charming medieval town of Metz, in northern France. Meanwhile, just outside Metz, and less charmingly, a piece of German mortar was blocking the airway of an American soldier who was, his companion recalled, “thrashing around” and “gasping for breath”. The companion acted swiftly. He took the soldier’s fountain pen and said “I don’t like to do this.” Then he slit the man’s throat open with a knife, popped the fountain pen in—and the soldier started to breathe. And a pen legend began.

There is, as Simon Garfield, a journalist, points out in an engaging new book, a pen paradox. Pens have changed the course of history, competing with “the wheel, steam engine and microchip” as “our most important tool of progress”. Pens make history in the literal sense (the second world war was postponed by pen in Munich; Hermann Göring, a Nazi, committed suicide using poison—possibly—smuggled into his cell in a pen). And pens make history in the literary sense too, since “to write history” is, as the philosopher Michael Oakeshott pointed out, “the only way of making it”.

Yet the pen has left surprisingly little mark on the page about itself. The earliest image of a quill does not appear until 547AD: an Italian mosaic in a church in Ravenna depicts St Matthew at his writing desk. Most artists and authors ignore their pens entirely except to grouse about them: Virginia Woolf hated her “disgusting, slippery, false” fountain pen; Jane Austen felt “sick of myself & my bad pens”. King Charles III harrumphed at his “bloody” pen. The pen is the handmaid of history: it writes history, then is written out of it.

This history more than makes up for that, as it follows the story of the pen (briefly) from pre-history then onwards, and in closer detail, as the pen becomes first a “necessity” and later an industry with billions of dollars in annual sales. But the arrival of the pen proper (in the sense of a hollow thing with a pointy metal bit at the end) was surprisingly late. People describe writers as “penning” their works—but for most of history they didn’t. Tang-dynasty poets used brushes; Cicero wrote his letters with a reed pen; Julius Caesar used a stylus, long and pointed as a nail, to write (and, ever the pragmatist, also used it to stab an assassin in the arm).

The pen proper only really began with the quill, from which the modern pen got its name (penna is Latin for “feather”). The word’s ubiquity reflects the quill’s: Shakespeare wrote the “words, words, words” of “Hamlet” with quills; Magna Carta was signed with one; the da-da-da-dum of Beethoven’s Fifth was drafted by one. Almost everyone was irked by them: the ink leaked; the nibs split (and had to be reshaped with a “penknife”); a busy writer could get through five in a day. This was annoying for them and not much fun for geese: in 1832 England imported 33.7m goose quills, mostly from the Netherlands and Germany.

It all changed in France in 1827 when someone had the idea—the “inkling”—of manufacturing something a bit more sturdy, with a well of ink inside and a metal nib. Or this may have happened in Iowa in 1867. Or Egypt in 970AD. Or possibly with Leonardo da Vinci in the 15th century. Whatever. Eventually everyone had one. Those who manufactured the most popular pens became—like Messrs Waterman, Parker and (later, with a different nib) Biro—household names. (Around 150bn Bics had been made by 2024.)

They also became a status symbol. Rorschach would claim that you could tell a personality from an ink splat; far more telling were the pens that ink came from. James Bond had an exploding pen. Freud allegedly had a huge red pen (prompting talk of “pens envy”).

Once omnipresent, the pen is now becoming obsolescent: “to pen” will perhaps soon join words like “scribe” and “manuscript” as a linguistic literary relic. This book—which could admittedly have done with a red pen through some of its longer sections—persuades the reader of what a shame that would be. ■
