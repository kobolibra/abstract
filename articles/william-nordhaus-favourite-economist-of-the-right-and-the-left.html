William Nordhaus, favourite economist of the right and the left

The Nobel-prizewinner was fascinated by what happens when markets fail

Nov 5th 2026

WILLIAM NORDHAUS had faith in the tools of his profession. For decades he helped run Yale University’s economics department, where he arrived in 1967, aged 26, and stayed until he died on October 30th. He edited “Economics”, a textbook still used by American undergraduates. And he was convinced that economic methods could be useful to other disciplines, leaving wonkish topics behind in pursuit of debates that consumed policymakers: the climate, technology and taxes.

His faith made him unflinching—and unafraid of a fight. No other economist of his generation worked across such a breadth of controversial topics. He pioneered carbon prices (which helped earn him a Nobel prize in 2018), pleasing America’s progressives and irking its conservatives. He also defended billionaires, with the opposite effect. As a result, he became a rare economist to win the respect of the left and right alike.

Economics relies on the price system. Mr Nordhaus spent his life calculating prices that are missing. His doctoral thesis examined such failures in the knowledge economy, and suggested the optimal patent life for inventions that minimises underinvestment in copyable ideas and maximises social welfare. In the 1970s he decided that building theoretical models of technological change was “too hard” (and left the task to Paul Romer, with whom he shared the Nobel). So he turned to climate change instead.

Although the practice of climate economics would in time become politically fraught, the theory was manageable. Economic growth depended on energy. Firms that produced and used energy were not bearing the cost of the resulting carbon pollution. The warming that would ensue could hurt growth. What to do? One research group at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology favoured abandoning growth altogether. Mr Nordhaus disagreed. The problem, he thought, was a lack of a model that brought together the scientific and economic equations to connect growth, emissions and the environment.

Mr Nordhaus spent the next two decades trying to construct one. In 1992 he published a paper outlining the “dynamic integrated climate economy” (DICE) model. Its more than 20 equations eventually ranged from basic economic ones he taught undergraduates to scientific predictions about the risk of catastrophes and forecasts about how humans would adapt to a hotter world. Throw in a discount rate, the value placed on the lives of future generations relative to the present, and the model churns out the path of emissions reductions that would not plunge millions into poverty today, as a result of insufficient energy production, or tomorrow, if global warming wrecked the economy. The carbon price which would set the world on that path equalises the cost of abating an extra tonne of CO2 and that of the damage this tonne would do in the future. As concerns about climate change mounted, so did interest in this work. By 2010 Barack Obama’s administration had incorporated DICE into American policy.

Mr Nordhaus’s climate economics had critics. He fought a long battle with Nicholas Stern, a British economist who favoured more weight on future lives. Some climate scientists thought he underestimated the probability of catastrophic events and contested the conclusion of his DICE paper that the economically optimal outcome was to allow average global temperature to rise by 3°C above pre-industrial levels. Yet even as they challenged some of his numerical assumptions, his detractors happily worked within his framework.

DICE made Mr Nordhaus a climate celebrity. But it was only the tip of a vast iceberg of academic output. A paper from 1975 suggested that politicians loosened monetary policy to bring down unemployment in the months before elections to win votes—and led to a proliferation of similar political-economy studies. In 1996 he compared the cost of generating the equivalent of an hour’s worth of light from a modern lightbulb over the ages. His conclusion, that it plummeted from 5.4 hours of a worker’s labour in 1800 to less than half a second in 1992, implied that conventional price indices vastly understate gains in living standards.

He eventually returned to the preoccupation of his doctoral days. In 2005 he used American records from 1948 to 2001 to calculate that inventors got just 4% of the value their inventions brought to society. The economic contributions of innovators, in other words, far exceeded their fortunes. The paper appeared as America was minting new tech billionaires, and its arguments were embraced by their defenders. If the self-made tycoons give so much to society, why tax them more? Even progressive Californians seem to agree: in midterm elections on November 3rd most of the state’s voters appear to have rejected a ballot measure to tax billionaires’ wealth.

Above all, Mr Nordhaus’s economics was a triumph of pragmatism. He believed in moulding messy problems into understandable frameworks—and embracing the implications, regardless of where they took you. Producing numbers that were useful, he thought, was sometimes more important than being precise. The best economic predictions, he argued in a paper in 1987, are the ones that incorporate the most information (his own informed projection made a decade before, that global temperatures would have risen by about 1°C by around now, was on the money).

He imparted openmindedness to his students at Yale, too, patiently taking their questions after lectures—often for hours given how packed these got. In another display of enlightened generosity, he presented the Nobel museum with the ancient Babylonian lamp he procured while investigating the cost of light. Beside his intellectual gifts to economics, this was but a flicker. ■
