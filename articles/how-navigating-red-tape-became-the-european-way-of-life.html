How navigating red tape became the European way of life

Life in the small-print continent

Nov 12th 2026

Europe has spent centuries searching for ways to unite its quarrelsome peoples. Christianity had a go, as did various emperors, to little avail. Esperanto never quite caught on. Football and the Eurovision Song Contest bring the place together for a few nights each year. Perhaps Europe’s common culture is found not in stadiums or concert halls, but oozing from its government offices: battling red tape is the thread that binds the continent together. While Europeans disagree on politics, cuisine and religion, all are united in living under the administrator’s ink-stained thumb. From Dublin to Athens there is a reassuring familiarity to being informed that the correct form is unavailable, that another office must be consulted (preferably by fax), then that the deadline expired yesterday. The European Union in Brussels, the spiritual home of the paperwork-industrial complex, is a fitting culmination of attempts to forge ever-closer union. Where mere emperors failed, the rubber-stamping Eurocrat has triumphed.

All polities spawn paperwork. (Your columnist, a former correspondent in India, can attest to the subcontinent’s own procedural prowess.) Yet Europeans have elevated petty officialdom from a necessity to a fine art. The creation of rules, then their punctilious enforcement, is the je ne sais quoi that helps give Europe its particular charm. Multiple tiers of government invent rules often grumpily enacted by another level. If there is such a thing as a European way of life, it involves filling out forms in triplicate.

Every European has a horror story to recount. Charlemagne is no exception. Tax authorities in Belgium wrote recently to inform him that he was not, in fact, the father of his three children. (Come and pick them up at your convenience, was the response.) Some years ago it was French tinpot functionaries who refused to believe that the Stanley Victor Gregory Pignal identified on your columnist’s passport was the same person as the Stanley Victor Grégory (note the accent) Pignal named on his children’s birth certificates. After months of haggling it turned out to be easier to redo an entire set of identity documents—expunging the stray accent—than to try to reason with the apostles of clerical pedantry.

Even when the bureaucracy runs smoothly, try running a business in this place. The EU’s 27 countries between them regulate entry into more than 5,700 professions—over 200 per country—from the commonplace to the obscure. Woe betide an aspiring tiler, mushroom expert or well-sinker who would like to set up shop in Hungary without negotiating an obstacle course of certificates. (Nearly a quarter of EU workers toil under a regime that requires a recognised qualification.) Because the regulations vary from one country to the next, a Polish electrical engineer is unlikely to be allowed to fix a fault in neighbouring Lithuania, say—so much for the EU’s vaunted single market. Each country has its own labour code. Luxembourg’s alone runs to 2,160 pages.

Some places, like Estonia, have tried to tame the paper beast, making digital filings the norm. Still, Europe increasingly feels like a land in which something is forbidden unless it is expressly authorised. Want to offer shoppers across-the-board discounts? That is verboten in a slew of EU countries unless the promotion falls in a few specific weeks of the year. A German school that wants to order textbooks for its pupils may have to put out the contract to public tender under EU rules. Fair enough. But because the price of books is fixed under German diktats, all bids from suppliers come back at precisely the same price—and the contract is then allotted by drawing lots. (Perhaps unsurprisingly, German businesses spend 7% of their turnover on satisfying bureaucrats’ lust for box-ticking.) Value-added tax, a European innovation that has since gone global, kicks up a rich harvest of administrative lunacy. In the Netherlands, rabbit food until last year got taxed at 9% while hamster owners needed to spring 21% (official reason: the hamster is definitely a pet, while the rabbit might one day become a meal). Different yoga classes still attract different VAT rates, too, depending on how much sweating is involved.

All the red tape has compounded the economic malaise that has befallen Europe. American venture capitalists guffaw at the continent’s quaintly medieval ways. In Germany startups hoping to raise money can be required to get a notary to read all the investment documents aloud word for word. Since the small print can run to dozens of pages, this can take hours, if not days (the recital is interrupted for bathroom breaks). Every planning application from Poland to Portugal is a bureaucratic odyssey that must take into account the mating habits of each local newt and snail, no matter the pressing need for new homes. A bid to increase the capacity of Dublin airport in 2023 ran to 7,000 pages. The whole thing had to be printed out for submission to the authorities—before being scanned again by the very same authorities.

The EU machinery in Brussels, the final boss of the continent’s norm-setting apparatus, was once painted as a prodigious purveyor of rules: tabloid tales of how it regulated the curvature of bananas still haunt the place. Recently it has tried to paint itself as a force for deregulation. In theory every cost imposed by a new edict must be matched by one that is being repealed. Confusingly, the very same measure is sometimes enacted and repealed at the same time, a novel case of Schrödinger’s regulation. This summer a green packaging law took effect, which requires any firm selling goods across borders—even tiny ones—to appoint up to 27 local representatives to oversee their packaging waste. EU-wallahs, having belatedly realised the madness of the scheme, have since tried to junk it. But the law has taken effect before its scrapping has been agreed; officials hope nobody enforces it instead. Even when Europe decides to cut red tape, it finds itself tied up in knots. ■
