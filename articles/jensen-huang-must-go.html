Jensen Huang must go

His should not be the only head to roll

Nov 12th 2026

Editor’s note: Jensen Huang resigned on November 12th.

Jensen Huang is a brilliant man—and a fool. The boss of Nvidia, the world’s leading chip designer and until recently its most valuable company, had an extramarital affair. His lover was an executive at Megaspeed, a little-known cloud-computing firm that has bought billions of dollars’ worth of Nvidia hardware. American officials think she is a Chinese agent, and that her Singapore-based company is a front for smuggling the politically sensitive chips into China.

As we went to press, Nvidia’s board was meeting amid reports that it was planning to oust Mr Huang. If it had yet to find the courage to sack him, fresh allegations of another affair—this time with a direct report—will surely help. His position is untenable, and he must go. The same is true for American officials implicated in the Megaspeed scandal.

Mr Huang’s admission of infidelity on November 8th followed months of rumours about his relationship with Alice Huang (no relation). It remains unclear at what point Nvidia’s boss knew that his government thought her to be a Chinese agent, or when the affair ended. Nonetheless, he showed a catastrophic failure of judgment. Romance with a customer creates an obvious conflict of interest. And given his position as the steward of an American technology that China desperately covets, Mr Huang should have been extra wary. Instead, he lied publicly about the nature of their relationship as recently as last month.

If it is true that he also had an affair with a subordinate, no self-respecting board could allow him to remain. And Mr Huang’s transgressions may prove to have been graver still. Questions swirl as to whether Nvidia’s chief counsel intervened last year in an investigation into Megaspeed by America’s Commerce Department, which is responsible for enforcing export controls. Mr Huang’s advocacy of looser chip controls, ostensibly to keep China hooked on American technology, now arouses suspicion, as does his championing of Chinese open-source models.

Those who have argued that Mr Huang should stay reason that he is essential to Nvidia, and that Nvidia is essential to America’s lead over China in AI. Yet it is precisely because Nvidia is so important that he cannot continue as its chief executive. It is in America’s long-term interests that there be penalties for egregious misconduct at the top of firms which are crucial to national security. And for all Mr Huang’s undoubted brilliance, the interests of Nvidia’s shareholders would hardly be served by a scandal-ridden chief executive facing a merry-go-round of investigations, including by the FBI.

That there is no obvious successor makes the board’s job unenviable, but not impossible. Mr Huang has been deeply involved in every aspect of his business, with as many as 60 executives reporting to him directly—a situation the board should not have allowed. No one has been prepared for the top job. Even so, an experienced executive such as Colette Kress, Nvidia’s finance chief, should be capable of stepping in to stabilise the business while a permanent successor, perhaps from outside, is appointed.

Mr Huang is not the only one to have shown dismal judgment in the Megaspeed debacle. The Commerce Department, under Howard Lutnick, has failed abjectly to stem the flow of America’s best chips to China. It appears to have allowed Nvidia to keep selling to Megaspeed even after concerns over smuggling were raised, and to have resisted calls by the Pentagon, the Treasury and others in the administration to blacklist the Singaporean firm. Like Mr Huang, Mr Lutnick has advocated weaker chip-export controls.

Alas, the secretary of commerce is unlikely to resign. But as Democrats take control of Congress, they will interrogate not only Mr Lutnick but also President Donald Trump, who has bought Nvidia shares and tapped the company to fund his new ballroom. For two years incoherent policy and lax enforcement have allowed China to gain ground in the race for a technology that may prove more powerful than nuclear weapons. With luck, that will now change.

For years Mr Huang has been courted by governments and treated as a rock star. Like many powerful leaders, including others in Silicon Valley, he came to believe that he was too important for the normal rules to apply to him. The same attitude can be found, in even larger doses, within the Trump administration. This culture of impunity must end. It has damaged one of America’s great corporate successes and undermined its national security. After Mr Huang's, other heads should roll, too. ■

Clarification (November 11th): An earlier version of this article mentioned that Cantor Fitzgerald, an investment bank run by Howard Lutnick’s sons, holds a large position in Nvidia. That stake reflects its asset-management and other activities on behalf of clients.
