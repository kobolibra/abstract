The fight over Republicans’ future has begun

After a staggering loss, the party is battling over whom to blame and how to move forward

Nov 12th 2026

AS THE DIRE results came in on November 3rd, young Republicans at Butterworth’s, a MAGA haunt a stone’s throw from the Capitol in Washington, sipped glumly on cocktails such as the “Senate Majority Martini” and “The Red Wave”. “This is historic”, noted one, “in the same way the Hindenburg was historic.”

Donald Trump has commandeered the Republican party for over a decade. It is now adrift, with developments since the election only adding to the confusion. On Capitol Hill, Republicans are moving in different directions. On November 10th House Republicans elected as their leader Jim Jordan of Ohio, a co-founder of the hard-right Freedom Caucus who sought to overturn Joe Biden’s election in 2020. In contrast, John Thune of South Dakota, a veteran senator who has clashed with President Trump, was easily re-elected as the party’s leader in the upper chamber, despite efforts by more right-wing lawmakers to delay that election.

The Nvidia scandal adds further uncertainty; the party is only beginning to grapple with questions of how the administration handled intelligence that Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s boss, was having an affair with an alleged spy. The president’s support of artificial intelligence had become a divisive issue among Republicans, even before this scandal erupted over the industry’s most important executive. Mr Trump’s dominance of the party has for years papered over its fissures. They are now being laid bare.

The president himself remains defiant. On November 6th Mr Trump told CBS that his deportation drive had been “too tough” and that he was open to a deal on immigration with Democrats. An hour later, as if to declare himself the most MAGA of all, he appointed Stephen Miller, a nationalist and immigration hardliner who spearheaded that drive, as his new chief of staff. Within MAGA, loyalists to the president remain. Sarah Huckabee Sanders, the governor of Arkansas, was among those to parrot Mr Trump’s line that he was not on the ballot: “I don’t think that this can be a line drawn against him.” A few MAGA media figures, namely Mark Levin and Laura Loomer, continue to support the Iran war and America’s relationship with Israel.

Yet all this compounds problems for the president, who is already vulnerable to critics blaming the midterm loss on his policies and hubris. Since the election a noisy group of MAGA purists have contended that Mr Trump let the movement down. On November 4th J.D. Vance, Mr Trump’s vice-president, did not blame Mr Trump by name, but took the stunning step of blaming his boss’s policies, including the war, a failure to lower costs and the relationship with Israel.

MAGA-purist vodcasters such as Tucker Carlson and Steve Bannon had already broken with Mr Trump over the war before the election; they were scathing after it. On November 5th Natalie Winters, the co-host of Mr Bannon’s “War Room” show, alleged that White House officials such as Howard Lutnick, the commerce secretary, had made “shady deals” and that “the actual grifters in the administration” were tarnishing “the entire MAGA movement”. She stopped short of implicating the president.

Mr Carlson called the results a “disaster”, on his show on November 4th. “You could mark it on your calendar,” he added: “Trumpism gone.” He blames war, corruption (Mr Trump was “vain and venal”) and the president’s decision to endorse flawed candidates. The administration had been too focused on fighting anti-Semitism, he argued, a stance he dubbed “Jewish lives matter”. Mr Levin, for his part, skewered Mr Carlson as a “loathsome creature” who had worked to “sabotage the president, the GOP and the midterm elections”.

As brawls continue within MAGA, Republicans’ old guard, severely diminished but still defiant, is hoping for a resurrection. “The American people have rejected our party’s shift toward economic populism,” asserted Mike Pence, Mr Trump’s vice-president in his first term, in an op-ed in the Wall Street Journal, glossing over the populism espoused by many Democratic candidates. Mitt Romney, the last pre-Trump Republican to have run for president, in 2012, offered a more sweeping critique on X, pointing to the war and corruption, as well as Mr Trump’s “weaponised justice, ICE brutality, mountainous deficits, hiding Epstein, abandoning allies and coddling enemies”.

Between the MAGA purists and the old guard is a more fluid category of Republican, who could be charitably called pragmatists. Ron DeSantis, the term-limited governor of Florida, failed to win the Republican presidential nomination in 2024. He now hopes the MAGA movement might give him a second look.

Consider Ted Cruz, who after Tuesday’s sweep is poised to be the last remaining Republican statewide office-holder standing in Texas. In his own election autopsy on his podcast, he paid lip service to the need to rebuild as a “true blue-collar party”, but pointed to a pre-Trump solution: Republicans had to be “real free-market conservatives”. Mr Cruz, a long-standing proponent of muscular foreign policy who once cheered on Mr Trump’s war, felt compelled to change tack after the election, saying that the war should have been “brief, decisive and over”.

More fights will break out. Even as Mr Vance appeals to the MAGA purists, many within the MAGAverse despise him, not least for his ties to Silicon Valley, where he worked before running for Senate. MAGA purists are vehemently opposed to the conflict in Iran, championed by Pete Hegseth, the secretary of war, who narrowly avoided impeachment on November 10th. Yet Mr Hegseth’s purge of supposedly woke generals from the Pentagon has earned him ardent fans within the right-wing “manosphere”.

The most explosive argument may be over Nvidia. Republican hawks had opposed Mr Trump’s decision, after being lobbied by Mr Huang, to ease export restrictions of Nvidia chips to China. On November 9th Mr Bannon repeated his allegation that Mr Huang is himself a spy.

More battles will emerge over why the White House did not respond to a classified report from Marco Rubio, Mr Trump’s secretary of state, warning that Mr Huang’s mistress might be a Chinese spy, alongside concerns over why Mr Lutnick failed to keep America’s advanced chips from China. The election is over. The Republican brawl is just beginning.■
