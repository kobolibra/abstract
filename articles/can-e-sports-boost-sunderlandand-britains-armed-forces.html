Can e-sports boost Sunderland—and Britain’s armed forces?

High hopes for the new British Arena

Nov 12th 2026

“This was just a wasteland.” Graeme Miller, who ran Sunderland City Council for six years until 2024, gestures out of the window of the newish and still sparkling City Hall building. The site lay vacant for nearly two decades after the Vaux brewery closed in 1999. Now City Hall stands at the centre of a fresh clutch of office buildings facing the River Wear.

Sunderland, once the world’s biggest shipbuilding hub, has been in decline for decades. Its last shipyard closed in 1988. Tucked on England’s north-east coast, the city of 290,000 sits third-bottom for earnings in a ranking of Britain’s 63 largest urban areas. It is dead last for businesses per person. Voters showed their discontent at local elections in May, handing the populist-right Reform UK 58 of the council’s 75 seats. Labour went from 54 councillors to five. “People still are not happy, even with all of this,” laments Mr Miller.

Might e-sports help give Sunderland a brighter future? Enter British Esports, founded in 2016 by Chester King, who also runs a health-care business, and chaired by Andy Payne, a veteran of the video-games industry. Their venture has set up shop in what was a vacant Audi dealership next to Sunderland Football Club’s Stadium of Light. It has also turned a row of town houses and connected office space into its National Esports Performance Campus, where touring gaming pros can stay.

The former car dealership has been transformed into a 15,000-square-foot (1,400 square metres) e-sports venue, dubbed the British Arena. (“We couldn’t believe no one had used the name before,” says Mr King.) On a clear Thursday evening in October the arena hosted its second event, relaying a Sunderland away match on its giant screen. The 250-seat venue is barely 100 metres from the 49,000-seat stadium, which provides a handy source of footfall on match days—and also highlights the gulf between e-sports and traditional ones.

Mr King wants the British Arena to be more than just an entertainment venue. He hopes it will help elite gamers learn to perform to a crowd, while supporting careers in hospitality, broadcasting and coaching, too. E-sports can teach skills in business and marketing to those who may not be drawn to formal education, says Gregg Hill, a lecturer in e-sports event management at the University of Sunderland. Being the home of British Esports will build Sunderland’s reputation as a forward-looking digital city, enthuses Liz St Louis, the local authority’s aspirationally titled director of smart cities and enabling services.

Sunderland Council is not the only part of the British state putting faith in e-sports. So are the armed forces, aware that Ukraine’s battlefields have swarmed with drones piloted by youths with PlayStation controllers. The British Arena’s first official event was the International Defence Esports Games, at which Canadian personnel beat France in Counter-Strike 2 and Britain took the Rocket League title.

Where once the armed forces recruited from the playing fields of Eton, they now see value in gamers. The Ministry of Defence formally recognises e-sports and last year installed a room of gaming PCs on the Royal Navy’s flagship aircraft-carrier.

Back at City Hall Mr Miller insists that Sunderland’s economy is on the up. Ocado, an online grocer, and Apexon, a technology-services firm, have taken space in the new offices. A takeaway app, Just Eat, is testing robot deliveries in the city.

So far, e-sports and Sunderland seem a good fit. The city gets an optimistic owner for underused buildings; British Esports gets low prices and a welcoming local authority. Not to mention revenue from all those football fans’ pre-match pints. ■
