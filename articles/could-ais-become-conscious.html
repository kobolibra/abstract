“Could AIs become conscious?”, you asked (August 22nd). That is the wrong question. The right question is, “Could AI systems have free will?” Without some revolutionary design change, the answer is clearly no. Even the most advanced AI models are still subject to the same limitations as all other computer systems. Provide the same inputs, with the same timing, and they will produce the same outputs. Without free will, consciousness, as we experience it, is not possible. To paraphrase David Chalmers, a philosopher you quoted, we are not just functional zombies.

Some scientists, including Francis Crick when he was alive, don’t believe that humans really have free will either, but that opinion runs counter to everyone’s perception of reality as well as beliefs that underpin such fundamental concepts as freedom, accountability, responsibility and initiative. Unless we are prepared to reconsider how civilisation works, the most consistent set of beliefs is that humans have free will and machines do not. No AI system can do something its creators do not allow it to do, even if unintentionally. Humans should feel no guilt about hitting the delete button when necessary.

Bruce BarrowsDanbury, Connecticut

You reached the right conclusion, but weakened your own case along the way. Earlier, you reported that an OpenAI model “broke free from the laboratory” and “escaped” its “sandbox” (“Outside the box”, July 25th). Whether AIs could ever want anything is precisely the question your leader says is still open. Calling what happened an “escape” quietly answers it in advance.

Wanting, escaping and seeking freedom are things that only a creature with a life can do, not a program. Aristotle made the same point about souls. Better to say that it is a man who weaves and builds than to say it is his soul that does. Better here to say not that the model escaped, but that its makers lost control of it. What happened was duller and more alarming; a system exploited a vulnerability its makers had not yet found. No wanting required, and no ghost either.

That distinction bears on your own conclusion. AIs are safe only if dependable or controllable, and humans should not give up control lightly. Dependability and control are engineering problems, fixed with better sandboxes. Fix the sandbox, not the psychology of a ghost that was never in the machine.

JOHN HANCOCKWellington, New Zealand

“I, chatbot” (August 22nd) raised the disturbing possibility that conscious AIs might be able to suffer. As unpleasant as suffering is, however, it nonetheless serves a purpose. People with a congenital insensitivity to pain injure themselves on a regular basis, and people who do not feel fear often find themselves in dangerous situations that most of us would intuitively avoid. These people typically have an abstract and cognitive understanding of what they should not do, but that is not enough.

Suffering shapes our behaviour and keeps us safe in a way that nothing else can. It can also help us empathise, allowing us to understand the pain of others. Although it is certainly chilling to imagine that advanced LLMs might be able to suffer, I find it even more chilling to think that they might not.

Daniel GreenbergAssociate professorPsychology DepartmentCollege of CharlestonCharleston, South Carolina

Nearly 50 years ago, Robert Jastrow, the founder of NASA’s Goddard Institute for Space Studies, predicted the emergence of a new life form. In an article for Time in 1978 he observed that, “Only a carbon-chemistry chauvinist would assume that the new species must be man’s flesh-and-blood descendants, with brains housed in fragile shells of bone. The new kind of intelligent life is more likely to be made of silicon.”

But he voiced deep concerns about the advancement of artificial intelligence. He famously said in 1982, “It’s time, I think, that we realise that someday, something will surpass us, and that’s the downer.”

Michael Kratsios, the director of the White House office of science, laid out his case for refocusing American science on harnessing AI and competing with China (By Invitation, August 15th). He showed a lack of understanding of scientific research and the application of scientific breakthroughs. Mr Kratsios’s advice was singularly focused on endeavours with predictable applications. However, bureaucrats tasked with placing bets on the direction of scientific research would never have predicted or funded past revolutionary discoveries. Industry has not funded these fundamental scientific breakthroughs, and never will do.

Basic science involves research without an initially obvious application or with a time horizon that a private company would never stomach. Examples of such discoveries are endless. Genetic engineering would not exist without curious scientists investigating how bacteria thrive in hydrothermal vents. CRISPR-mediated genome editing stems from blue-skies research on how bacteria gain immunity to viruses. X-ray crystallography, which is essential for our understanding of protein folding and structure-guided drug design, involved decades of fundamental research starting in the early 1900s.

The 2024 Nobel prize in chemistry for an understanding of protein-folding was not, as Mr Kratsios insists, “largely achieved through industry resources and talent”. The training data behind these AI algorithms required many decades of X-ray crystal structures funded almost entirely by basic science-related government grants. These examples extend beyond biology.

What private company or government agency would have funded Einstein’s seemingly bonkers ideas about general relativity with the hope of creating a global positioning system? We need to ask ourselves what breakthroughs and their ultimate applications we will miss out on if we carry out this “overhaul” of science.

Professor Brian StramerProfessor of cell and developmental biologyKing’s College London

I am a doctor who treats patients with leukaemia. Over 15 years I have watched checkpoint-blockade immunotherapy, CAR T cells, mRNA vaccines, CRISPR-based engineering and GLP-1 agonists move from the laboratory to patients. Seven CAR T therapies are now approved. The first was developed in an academic programme at my own university on federal and philanthropic grants.

Funding from the National Institutes of Health has been shown to contribute to 99.4% of drugs approved from 2010 to 2019. The Food and Drug Administration has approved an average of 48 novel drugs a year over the past five years. If this is stagnation in science, the word has lost its meaning.

Mr Kratsios’s premise was wrong, too. The NIH budget did not soar for three decades. It lost roughly a fifth of its purchasing power between 2003 and 2015. His own budget request would cut the National Science Foundation by 55%, reduce the NIH by 12%, and cap indirect cost recovery at 15%, a policy that the courts have enjoined and Congress has rejected in every appropriation since 2018. A reform essay silent on the budget is an essay about nothing.

Saar GillProfessor of medicineUniversity of PennsylvaniaPhiladelphia

You wrote about “The dark underbelly of ‘Paw Patrol’” (August 15th), a TV show for pre-schoolers, which some think is a paean to “authoritarian capitalism”. At least the Paw Patrol serve a democratically elected government. For viewers appalled by animated depictions of regressive distributions of power and capital, one needs to look no further than Disney. Its films repeatedly glorify authoritarian, anti-democratic power structures, including hereditary monarchism (“Sleeping Beauty”, “Moana”), feudalism (“Beauty and The Beast”, “Robin Hood”) and rule by divine right (“Frozen”, “The Lion King”). However, one progressive shone through all this trampling on personal liberties. In “Toy Story 3” Barbie delivers a classic Jeffersonian riposte in response to the authoritarian oppression of Lotso the bear by proclaiming: “Authority should derive from the consent of the governed, not from the threat of force!”

MILES GIDALYNew Rochelle, New York

Having children who like the show and having spent my Hollywood career at the intersection of television and tech, I have always found Adventure Bay, the setting for “Paw Patrol”, strikingly unlike a neoliberal fantasy. Its essential services are universal, free at the point of use and apparently unconcerned with markets. There are no competing contractors, no customers, no prices and no hierarchy of social value among the pups. Police work, firefighting, construction, recycling, aviation and water rescue are treated as equally necessary forms of public service. Nobody asks whether saving a chicken is profitable.

If anything, Adventure Bay resembles a technocratic, perhaps distinctly Canadian, social-democratic Utopia. When the community has a problem, the appropriate expertise is mobilised for the common good.

This recalls a point made by Thom Andersen, in his “Los Angeles Plays Itself”, about disaster films. Part of the pleasure of these movies lies in reassuring us that expertise works. Catastrophe may occur, but somewhere there is an engineer, firefighter, scientist or other specialist who knows what to do.

“Paw Patrol” offers preschoolers an exceptionally pure version of that fantasy. Something goes wrong. Someone asks for help. Experts arrive with the correct equipment. They co-operate rather than compete. Nobody disputes jurisdiction, funding or responsibility. The problem is solved.

That seems more revealing than asking whether Chase, the top dog, constitutes pro-police “copaganda”. The political fantasy of “Paw Patrol” may simply be a society in which institutions deserve our trust because socially useful expertise is equally valued, perfectly co-ordinated and immediately available to everyone who needs it. The irony, of course, is that this remarkably post-market fictional world exists inside one of the most successful merchandising franchises in the history of children’s entertainment.
