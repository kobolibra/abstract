Explaining the popularity of Max Richter’s compositions

The classical composer manages to spin even serious subjects into gold

Nov 5th 2026

DOES ANY musician get around as much as Max Richter? In April he will be in Las Vegas, providing musical direction for Oprah Winfrey’s “immersive symphony of senses” at the Sphere. He is on screens: his soundtrack for “Hamnet” was nominated for an Academy Award this year. And he may well be in your bedroom: “Sleep”, his eight-and-a-half-hour-long composition, has been streamed more than 2bn times.

But it is probably through “On the Nature of Daylight”, a soulful six-minute composition for string quintet from 2004, that Mr Richter (pictured) has most successfully colonised the consciousness of listeners. It heightens emotions in at least a dozen films and television programmes, including “My Brilliant Friend” and “Arrival”. It transforms millions of commutes into occasions for reverie. Listeners will hear echoes of it on “While the Flood Rages”, a new album.

Achieving such ubiquity would be a feat for any composer. That Mr Richter writes classical music makes it more remarkable. Born in Germany and brought up in Britain, Mr Richter studied at the Royal Academy of Music in London and with Luciano Berio, an Italian modernist. If you do not detect the classical pedigree in Mr Richter’s music, he may point it out to you. In an interview at his studio in the Cotswolds he relocates from the sofa to the piano to demonstrate that a falling sequence of notes from his new album is reminiscent of the music of Johann Sebastian Bach. His record label is Decca, among the most storied in classical music.

But the genre would confine Mr Richter if he did not bend it. Kraftwerk, a German electronic band, is an influence alongside American minimalists such as Philip Glass. The piano in his studio keeps company with banks of vintage synthesisers, giving it the appearance of “mission control”, he jokes. As is fashionable, he deems the conventional distinction between classical music and other sorts to be “bizarre”.

Mr Richter’s medley of influences and instincts have landed him in a musical sweet spot shared with few contemporary composers. Thanks in part to his classical chops, he collaborates with choreographers as well as film directors. “While the Flood Rages” draws from music he wrote for a ballet, “MaddAddam”, which in turn is inspired by a post-apocalyptic trilogy by Margaret Atwood, teller of “The Handmaid’s Tale”. In April the Orchestre de Paris will play the premiere of a cello concerto Mr Richter is writing.

His music has an emotional directness that gives it much wider appeal than that of most living classical composers. Mari Samuelsen, a Norwegian violinist who often performs it, finds it “extremely meditative and cleansing”, both “simple and complex at the same time”. Although film and phone are its natural habitat, Mr Richter’s music also packs concert halls. Audiences tend to be younger than at conventional classical concerts, she says, and more prone to share reactions on social media.

To some listeners the sweet spot is too sweet. Some think his partnerships with brands such as Krug champagne and Christian Dior, a fashion house, are evidence that his art lulls rather than challenges. Alex Ross, a prominent critic, has written that Mr Richter’s pieces “exude a gentle fatalism, a numbed acquiescence” even in the face of “global disaster”. Many deal explicitly with political themes. “Voices”, from 2020, incorporates text from the UN human-rights declaration. “Sleep”, Mr Richter has said, is a “protest” against a “very super-industrialised, intense, mechanised way of living”. “Daylight” comes from an album that opposes America’s war in Iraq. Yet most of the time the strife seems to be happening in the distance.

“While the Flood Rages” considers the aftermath of apocalypse, environmental vandalism and corporate greed. Mr Richter says the music is “more highly coloured” than what he normally composes, echoing the “stylistic omnivorousness” of Ms Atwood’s writing. Synthetic and recorded sounds—of water, of gunfire—suggest a world made strange and threatening. But Mr Richter’s sensibility has the last word. Piano, strings and a church-like choir, sometimes in counterpoint with eerier music, seem to suggest that something good will prevail. The listener, unsettled at first by the album’s dystopian theme, may come away consoled.

That is often the effect of Mr Richter’s music. Rather than depict evil, he imagines alternatives to it. “We’re flooded with negativity, with negative images and negative storytelling,” largely because of social media, he says. “I’m looking to celebrate and elevate the things that are not that, which are ordinary…human qualities which have been with us for ever and will continue to be with us, but are easy to overlook.” While floods rage, Mr Richter offers audiences a life raft. No wonder he’s so in demand. ■
