Can Jesus Christ sell moderates on Democrats? 

Donald Trump’s faltering support among Christians creates an opportunity

Oct 1st 2026

For a sense of how Christianity features in American politics, consider a video shown at the Republican Party’s recent midterm convention in Dallas. “On June 14th 1946”, it begins, “God looked down on this planned paradise and said, ‘I need a caretaker’.” So, the video went on, “God gave us Trump.” The video continued in this line, explaining God’s need for somebody to “get up before dawn”, “eat supper” and “fight the Marxists”.

Now a rather different Christian message is being preached on the campaign trail. “What we see that purports to be Christian in Washington does not represent the Jesus of the Gospels,” says Adam Hamilton, the Democratic nominee for the Senate in deeply Republican Kansas. Mr Hamilton, a pastor, runs the largest United Methodist church in America, with a congregation of 24,000. He is one of the most surprising candidates in these midterms.

Though still an underdog, Mr Hamilton now has a 42% chance of defeating the Republican incumbent, Roger Marshall, according to The Economist’s election model. That is so alarming to Republicans that J.D. Vance, the vice-president and author of a recent book on faith, recently visited the state to stump against him. By emphasising his faith, Mr Hamilton may have found a route to voters disappointed by Mr Trump but still sceptical of Democrats.

White evangelical voters have been a core constituency of Republican candidates for decades, mobilised in part by their belief that abortion should be illegal. With Roe v Wade overturned in 2022, the focus of Christian voters has become more fuzzy, and campaign messages less clear. Mr Trump claims divine purpose—“I was saved by God to make America great again,” he said at his inauguration last year. Mr Vance’s faith underpins a broader effort to affirm America’s Christian identity. An ascendant wing of Christian nationalists questions the separation of church and state, as well as women’s right to vote. What does not feature much is helping the poor or turning the other cheek.

Christians began losing their faith in Mr Trump in the first year of his second presidency, according to the Pew Research Centre. The biggest fall was approval among non-evangelical white Protestants, which dropped from 46% to 33%. Yet even among evangelicals, approval slid from 66% to 58%. Just 40% of evangelicals said they were confident Mr Trump was acting ethically in office. A growing number of evangelical pastors have broken away from the Republican Party, too. Criticism sharpened in April, when Mr Trump posted an AI-created picture that seemed to depict himself as Jesus. (The president claimed that he was merely “a doctor”.)

For Democrats, this creates an opportunity, with Mr Hamilton part of an ascendant group of Christian leaders. In Texas James Talarico, a Presbyterian seminary student and former teacher, has a good chance of winning what had hitherto been a solidly Republican Senate seat. Three more mainline Protestant clergy are running for the House.

Mr Hamilton, a former Republican, explains that he entered the race because he was so upset by what he says is cruelty and indecency wrought by some Christians around Mr Trump. “Jesus of the gospels was concerned with the poor,” he explains to The Economist. “He was concerned with people who were hungry and thirsty—and strangers, immigrants even.”

Though Mr Hamilton describes himself at every opportunity as “a moderate or independent-minded Democrat”, his policy positions are rather conventional. He criticises the White House for cutting food stamps and support for health care. He emphasises the importance of immigration and the cruelty of deporting longtime residents. He stops short of saying that Immigrations and Customs Enforcement should be abolished, but he does say it has been “militarised and politicised” and needs radical change. In some ways, that makes his message similar to that of Mr Talarico. But unlike the Texan candidate, he avoids invective against the wealthy. Last year Mr Talarico told The Economist that the gospels are populist. Mr Hamilton, by contrast, is keen to say he is a capitalist.

In Kansas, this strategy seems to be working. At an event on September 20th in Lawrence, home to the University of Kansas, over 150 people crowded into a gallery to hear Mr Hamilton speak. The vast majority were middle-aged white voters; all but one of those interviewed by The Economist identified as Republicans. Several spoke with reverence about Bob Dole, a famed Republican senator and presidential candidate from Kansas. Among the registered Republicans present was Deanell Tacha, a retired appeals-court judge appointed to the bench by Ronald Reagan.

This makes Republican politicians anxious. In Kansas Mr Vance accused Mr Hamilton of supporting what he called a “devil worshipping” gay-rights group that puts on drag shows for children (the basis for the accusation is that Mr Hamilton’s church was a sponsor of a gay-pride rally). Mr Trump has called the pastor a “radical left lunatic” and Mr Marshall has falsely alleged that Mr Hamilton said Americans should repent for eating beef, an incendiary view in cattle country.

Mr Hamilton contends that this shows the success of his message. “If they’re going to come and attack your faith, that’s pretty low, really,” he argues. At a debate at the Kansas State Fair on September 12th, Mr Marshall said that Mr Hamilton is “too woke and too weak” for Kansas. “I’m pretty sure if Jesus were here,” he responded, “you would call him woke.”■
