The City of London honours an ancient pact

Meet the world’s most elaborate rent payment

Oct 15th 2026

In a fusty room at the Royal Courts of Justice, a man in a tricorn hat receives a payment. He is the king’s remembrancer, an office once charged with keeping track of money owed to the crown. This is the annual Ceremony of Quit Rents, held this year on October 14th. The earliest recorded one was in 1211. Each year the City of London pays a levy to the monarch for two plots of land: six giant horseshoes, 61 nails and two knives, one blunt and one sharp.

The remembrancer then inspects the wares. An official tries the blunt knife—traditionally a billhook—which should be able to bend a hazel stick without cutting it. Next he wields the sharp one, which needs to chop through the wood. Once the horseshoes and nails have been counted, the payment is accepted and the City can enjoy its tenancies for another 12 months.

In theory, at least. These days no one knows exactly where the rented land is, though some have made educated guesses. The first parcel is somewhere in Shropshire, where a man named Nicholas rented moorland before passing his tenancy on. The other is near London’s Strand, a stone’s throw from The Economist’s office. It came from a blacksmith called Walter, who paid for access to a forge (hence the horseshoes). Uncertainty over the postcodes has not stopped the crown claiming its due. In today’s Square Mile firms hire armies of lawyers to draft agreements and then wriggle out of them. But for the king’s remembrancer contracts are sacred.

The ceremony is the sort of thing Britain is famous for. In this common-law country, precedent piles on precedent and keeping up appearances is paramount. Hence the Tower of London has been locked at 9.53pm every night for 700 years, and the king has held an annual swan-counting event for even longer.

Other “quit rents” abound. For centuries the City has paid the crown £11 ($15) a year for the “town of Southwark”. In parts of East Anglia, Leicester and London people honour ancient deals by handing over roses. The Duke of Wellington hangs onto his country mansion only so long as he delivers a French flag to the king each June (something to do with Waterloo).

The autumn ceremony at the Royal Courts of Justice is the most ancient such occasion. Those watching from the public gallery admit the event is nonsense. But, says one, it is “lovely nonsense”. ■
