Are small planes Britain’s next small boats?

The problem is that nobody knows

Oct 8th 2026

Dinghies crossing the channel, laden with asylum-seekers, have turned Britain’s maritime border into a national obsession. But with all eyes on the coast, who is watching the skies?

A new study from RUSI, a defence think-tank, argues that “criminals are exploiting a border-security blind spot” in general aviation—a category that covers everything from hot-air balloons to helicopters. Small planes and private jets are a trafficker’s dream. In Britain, their comings and goings are difficult to monitor, according to Cathy Haenlein, the report’s author.

Such aircraft have to submit a General Aviation Report (GAR) declaring passenger information and other details. With over 77,000 arrivals each year, the vast majority legitimate, good data are vital for identifying suspicious flights. But pilots can put in a GAR as little as two hours before departure, leaving little time to assess it. Unless the GAR contains obvious red flags, traffickers could easily encounter no customs officials when they land.

The Home Office says all high-risk flights are targeted for checks. But in 2024 London City Airport—much better resourced than most small airfields—was found to be failing to meet this goal. Britain has 538 airfields able to receive international flights, some in remote areas. Scrambling a team to meet a suspicious flight can therefore be a challenge. Worse, pilots can amend flight plans after take-off.

In 2018 some 500kg of cocaine, worth £41m ($55m), was found on a private jet arriving in Hampshire. Europol warned 15 years ago that light aircraft were being used to move diamonds across Europe. Police are assessing whether Jeffrey Epstein used private jets to traffic women to and from Britain.  Yet Britain lacks figures on such crimes. Border Force does not reveal how many flights it labels as high-risk, or subjects to extra checks. As one official says: “We’re not seeing it because we’re not looking for it.” Advances in drone technology promise further challenges.

David White, of the Light Aircraft Association, is more sanguine. Project Pegasus, set up by law enforcement, allows airfield users to report suspicious activity. Mr White says they are keen to use it, lest a few bad apples encourage overbearing regulation. “You might get away with it once,” he says, but questionable trips will quickly draw attention. “We’re nosey like that.” ■
