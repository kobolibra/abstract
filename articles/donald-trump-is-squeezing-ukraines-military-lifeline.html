Donald Trump is squeezing Ukraine’s military lifeline

To protect his Russian diesel deal, he appears to be withholding vital military intelligence

Oct 15th 2026

UKRAINE’S AIR-raid sirens are the final link in a ballistic warning system that usually begins with American satellite or signals intelligence. They give citizens a few minutes to brace for an attack. But on the nights of October 12th and 13th scores of Russian missiles slammed into Kyiv with little or no warning. Ukraine said there had been no intelligence from its partners. In an interview published in the Financial Times on October 15th, Volodymyr Zelensky, Ukraine’s president, accused America of cutting it off.

American officials have threatened to do so since Donald Trump’s announcement on October 9th of a deal to let Russia sell diesel fuel. They demand that Ukraine stop attacking Russian refineries. Arms supplies are at risk as well. The Americans have privately warned that they may pull the plug on the Prioritised Ukraine Requirements List (PURL), the mechanism by which European countries pay for American weapons sent to Ukraine.

Mr Trump insisted on October 13th that intelligence sharing was continuing. Yet Ukrainian officials soon reported that Elon Musk’s SpaceX had restricted the Starlink terminals used aboard Ukrainian attack drones. The terminals began to lose connectivity at speeds of around 160kph, forcing some units to cancel attacks. Mr Musk said it was America’s armed forces that have control over those contracts.

It all added to the grim mood in Kyiv. Amid drone and missile barrages and power cuts, there is no sign of the “energy ceasefire” Mr Trump claims to have brokered between Ukraine and Russia. In a neighbourhood on the city’s left bank on October 14th residents of a nine-storey apartment block collected plywood to board up windows shattered by a ballistic missile. Russian strikes have killed well over 100 civilians so far this month.

Mr Zelensky says he will accept an energy ceasefire if Russia does. To appease Mr Trump, Ukraine suspended attacks on Russian energy targets for three days. But Russia struck dozens of Ukrainian energy facilities during the supposed truce. Mr Zelensky told the Financial Times that attacks on Russian refineries will resume.

The Starlink limits are a serious blow. Ukrainian troops use the system to guide drones in Russian-occupied territory. (The system does not work inside Russia itself.) “Almost all of our mid-range strike capabilities rely on Starlink,” says a Ukrainian military-intelligence source. The timing of the restrictions “cannot be a coincidence”, Serhii Beskrestnov, an adviser to Mr Zelensky, wrote on Telegram.

Ukraine can snoop for itself, using commercial satellite imagery, but its feeds are not as rich as America’s. America also lets Ukraine use cutting-edge artificial-intelligence tools to crunch data. Europe cannot replicate this. When Mr Trump briefly blocked intelligence sharing last year, Britain and France were unable to guide the cruise missiles they had given to Ukraine. And cutting off American intel would badly hurt Ukraine’s ability to shoot down Russian missiles. “Nobody in the world has those capabilities,” says a Ukrainian air-defence specialist.

Ending PURL would also be devastating. Ukraine depends on the programme for 95% of its Patriot interceptors and for many of the missiles its F-16 fighter jets use to shoot down jet-powered drones. The next shipment is reportedly imminent, so Ukraine will know soon if it is blocked. Some Patriot interceptors are coming from European countries, as well as a new generation of SAMP/T air-defence systems from France and Italy. But “PURL is the only solution we have to protect critical infrastructure,” says the air-defence officer.

Dependence on America makes desisting from strikes on Russian oil infrastructure seem the obvious choice. But it is a potent tactic to give up. Burning refineries grab the world’s attention and raise morale. Fuel shortages force Russians to stop ignoring the war. And the damage is cutting into the Russian state’s oil revenues. Our analysis this summer showed that those had fallen 34% short of what would be expected, based on global oil prices.

There are other valuable targets that Ukraine can hit. Figures from Armed Conflict Location & Event Data, a conflict monitor, show only 21% of Ukraine’s strikes into the Russian heartland were on oil-related facilities. Besides data centres and retail warehouses, the vast majority were military-industrial. These are the factories supporting Russia’s war machine, producing the 28,000 jet-powered attack drones it expects to build this year.

There are no practical alternatives to what America provides. Only China and Russia are close to a Starlink equivalent. Europe is years away from American levels of missile production. For all Ukraine’s extraordinary resilience and inventiveness, its war effort depends on outside support.

Ukraine has no alternative to American sensors and satellites, which map Russian spoofing, jamming, and troop movements, helping the Ukrainians plan and conduct drone attacks. Ending such co-operation would cripple Ukraine’s ability to take the fight to Russia. “From the very beginning of any mission we design, there is a really critical element of [American] intelligence,” says a Ukrainian intelligence source. European allies have expanded their own satellite-surveillance capacity, but “the Americans can see inside Russia 24 hours and seven days a week,” says the source. “Without this [intel] exchange, it would be really hard, almost impossible, for us to fight.”■
