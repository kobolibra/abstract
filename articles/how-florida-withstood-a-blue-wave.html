How Florida withstood a blue wave

The quintessential swing state of 2000 is the bastion of Republicanism in 2026

Nov 12th 2026

“TO CALL IT a wave I don’t think would be fully accurate,” Ron DeSantis, the governor of Florida, said the morning after Democrats won control of Congress. “It’s the political equivalent of a meteor strike that went all around the United States—blue states, red states, everything in between. And the one state that was able to hold the line and withstand that is right here in the free state of Florida.”

Indeed, even as Democrats popped champagne in states that had once been deep crimson, Republicans in Florida were elected to all open statewide offices. They held their congressional seats and retained control of both legislative chambers. They were not immune to Mr Trump’s unpopularity—they lost their supermajority in the legislature and, compared with 2024, Florida swung 10.2 percentage points to the left. But that was less than the national average of 15 points, and far below swings of 20 points or more in Texas, Georgia, South Carolina and Mississippi, where Democrats flipped crucial seats. Florida, long since evolved from being a swing state, is Republicans’ most defiant stronghold.

There are three main reasons Florida was spared the uprising against Republicans. The first is demographics, an advantage that Republicans elsewhere will struggle to mimic. For six years Florida politicians have advanced policies, from rejecting covid lockdowns to enacting universal school vouchers, to make the state a sanctuary for MAGA refugees. Although Democrats turned out a higher share of their voters this year, they could not overpower Republicans, who came into the election with 1.5m more registered voters. It helped Republicans that Hispanics, who propelled swings in other red states, were less keen on Democrats in Florida. Cubans make up half the state’s Hispanic voters and, unlike Mexicans or Puerto Ricans, have been fused to the Republican Party since the days of Ronald Reagan. Exit polls show they moved left but didn’t flip.

The second reason for Republicans’ success is that—unlike in Texas, Kansas or Iowa— Democrats in Florida ran mediocre candidates who struggled to raise money. In the race to succeed Mr DeSantis, who was term-limited, Byron Donalds, the Republican nominee, outraised David Jolly, his Democratic opponent, $173m to $27m; Mr Jolly managed his own campaign, refused to debate and, insiders say, was allergic to asking for cash. In Florida’s Senate race, Angie Nixon, a member of the Democratic Socialists of America, surprised Democrats by winning the nomination in August, and continued to surpass naysayers’ expectations. But whereas James Talarico in Texas and Annie Andrews in South Carolina campaigned on messages of unity, Ms Nixon was known for yelling at her Republican colleagues in the legislature with a pink bullhorn. In the general election she succeeded in driving turnout, including of voters who opposed her.

National Democrats refused to cough up much cash themselves, both because they doubted some candidates and because running ads in Florida is so expensive—supporting a statewide candidate means placing ads across ten media markets and appealing to an electorate so diverse it can feel like Manhattan and rural Alabama stitched together. Where the party did spend, in four Democratic congressional districts gerrymandered to favour Republicans, candidates held on. “Going forward Democrats can either invest or continue dying in Florida,” says Fernand Amandi, a Democratic strategist in Miami.

The third reason that Florida Republicans thrived is that their message reflected the politics of 2026, not 2024. David Johnson, a Republican operative in Tallahassee, had warned candidates against fighting culture wars, largely because Republicans in Florida had already won them—the state had banned firms with state contracts from promoting diversity, and transgender women from competing in women’s sports in public schools. On the campaign trail Mr Donalds duly emphasised affordability, promising to cut insurance and health-care costs to preserve the “Florida dream”. Mr DeSantis contends that Republicans elsewhere had lost the plot.

Which Republican politician might offer a model of how to do better? Mr DeSantis, of course, at least according to him. “The most dangerous place in Florida last week was between Ron and a Fox News camera,” says a Republican strategist. A massive property-tax cut that Republicans put on the ballot, Mr DeSantis says, gave their base a reason to turn out. Even as Washington failed to deliver what Mr DeSantis calls “bold conservative change”, the governor managed to halve the state’s debt and shrink its government to make the economy boom. “Florida shows how you can build a coalition not only to convert on all your promises in good times but also to withstand the bad times,” he declared on Fox News on November 4th.

That same day, Mr DeSantis’s odds of becoming the Republican presidential nominee in 2028 jumped from 5% to 18% on Kalshi, a betting website. The last time Mr DeSantis ran for president, in 2024, his campaign flopped, his robotic delivery no match for Mr Trump’s enduring appeal. Now Mr DeSantis’s narrative is sufficiently compelling that Republican voters will give him another chance, argues Steve Vancore, a Florida pollster.

The trouble with narratives is that they can change. Mr DeSantis has much to boast about now, but some of his policies may not age well. The measure to slash the state’s property taxes on primary residences may look less like a victory by 2028, when towns in Florida are slashing budgets for police and fire departments. Mr DeSantis admits that the state, which has no income tax, may need a trust fund to help local governments. In 2026, Florida has become America’s most important red state. If these midterms have taught America anything, though, it is that no politics are permanent.■
