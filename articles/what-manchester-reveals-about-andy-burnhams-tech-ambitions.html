What Manchester reveals about Andy Burnham’s tech ambitions

His two priorities sit awkwardly together

Sep 17th 2026

FORGIVE TECH bosses for thinking that Andy Burnham, Britain’s prime minister, is sending mixed messages. In one of his first speeches, he talked about making Britain the “world’s leading innovation nation”. Yet one of his earliest actions was to dismantle their front door to government, the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology. Rumours (since denied) have circulated of an exit tax for startup founders who leave the country. His team seem reluctant to remove regulatory speed bumps that slow the roll-out of British-designed driverless cars.

Perplexed executives can learn from Mr Burnham’s record before he became prime minister. His spell as mayor of Greater Manchester between 2017 and June 2026 makes his stance on tech clearer. It also suggests it may not work as advertised.

Mr Burnham’s “Manchester model” rests on a simple idea: that inclusive growth, driven by innovation, is best achieved when business, academia and local government work together. Frontier technologies are great, provided they give local people good jobs. One of his signature projects is Atom Valley, a planned manufacturing and tech hub that would turn scientific discoveries, especially graphene, a form of carbon discovered in Manchester, into high-value jobs in the comparatively poor boroughs of Bury, Oldham and Rochdale. Another is Sister, the city centre’s latest regeneration project for a new £1.7bn ($2.3bn) innovation district.

This model can attract tech firms. Circle Square, a glitzy tech hub, has drawn some big-name brands such as Hewlett Packard Enterprise, a business-software giant, and Autocab, part of Uber, a ride-hailing firm. Fergal O’Connor, the boss of Buymedia, an Irish advertising firm that uses artificial intelligence, says he chose to open its first foreign office in Manchester for its Irish-like community feel. Mr Burnham gave the company his backing. In his first speech as chancellor on September 7th, John Healey hailed the model for creating new clusters of innovation.

Still, it would be too much to suggest that a thriving local tech sector is driving growth. Greater Manchester has grown faster than the national average in the last decade (a modest achievement given that Britain’s long-run growth rate hovers just above 1%). Yet this seems to reflect transport improvements more than dynamic Mancunian tech. Two of the city’s biggest tech firms are Autotrader, an online car marketplace, and ncc Group, a cyber-security company whose stock price has fallen by 55% in five years and which struggles to compete with its American rivals. Tech remains a tiny fraction of economic output in the metropolis.

Mr Burnham might argue that promising firms have been starved of opportunity relative to those in the “golden triangle” of Cambridge, Oxford and London. With the extra levers he controls as prime minister, he plans to narrow regional inequalities by devolving more power to regional mayors and doling out extra money to northern scale-ups. At a national level, he speaks of championing scrappy British firms over American giants. His ministers talk of making more chips and drones at home, acting as an early customer for promising British tech firms and keeping promising companies in Britain for longer.

None of these interventions tackle Britain’s biggest problems. It will be almost impossible to stop technology firms leaking to America if British companies continue to face a dearth of late-stage capital, a shortage of compute and some of the world’s highest energy prices.

Besides, a successful British tech policy may widen the very regional divides that Mr Burnham wants to close. Real gdp grew by a better-than-expected 0.4% in the three months to July 2026, led by computer programming and consultancy activities that are predominantly in London and the South East. Those regions are also home to two-thirds of British ai companies. Agglomeration effects mean that growth is likely to occur there first. If Mr Burnham is to come good on his promise to help British tech, he must accept that many of the benefits will arise in the south.■
