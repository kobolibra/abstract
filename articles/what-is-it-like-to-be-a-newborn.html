What is it like to be a newborn?

Nobody can quite say. But a new book tries to figure it out

Nov 5th 2026

The naked creature in the incubator measures perhaps 50 centimetres and weighs some 900 grams. It scowls and twitches but cannot smile. Its skin is almost transparent, and its veins close to invisible; it will take a doctor several minutes first to find one and then to draw a few drops of blood. It is probably intubated to help breathing; another tube trickles milk directly to the stomach. If you put tape on this fragile being, you will tear its skin. But if you leave it, it will cry.

Clémentine Goldszal, a French journalist, is consumed by this image and by a single imperative: to find out what birth is like for newborns, and how they experience the multiple medical interventions that may, in a worst case, immediately follow. She would interview them if she could. But of course there is no communication. When her own son Lucien was born, what she took as sweet kittenish sounds turned out to be signs of acute respiratory distress. An apparently lively baby may, in fact, be having convulsions.

She decided to “construct a phenomenology of the newborn” by spending many months as an observer in the neonatal unit of the Necker in Paris. This is a superb hospital, especially for its care of premature babies, rated seventh in the world. Sick babies are rushed there from all over France. Standing in a corner of the incubator rooms, out of the way, she sees nurses ceaselessly watching the beeping monitors, checking on breathing and feeding. In other rooms she watches operations so delicate, performed with miniature instruments, that a margin of error of a millimetre would be a catastrophe.

What she wanted to decipher was the attitude of medical staff to the newborn: whether or not they considered them fully human, individual and even having personalities. She had viewed Lucien as merely “a generic entity” for some weeks, and found it hard to say his name. But at Necker newborn patients were all called by their names and treated tenderly. They were assumed to have agency, if only to close their eyes or poo in their nappies. When those nappies were changed, blankets were put over the incubators to shield their privacy. After all, in French law, they were individuals as soon as they drew breath and no longer occupied their mother’s womb.

And yet without their consent, these babies were invaded. They were injected, intubated, placed among noisy machines and blinded by harsh lights. Tiny catheters were sent in to deliver fluids. In France 94% of babies born between 27 and 31 weeks left the hospital in perfect health. (At 25 weeks, though, the figure fell to 60%.)

These numbers were improving steadily, and had been doing so ever since the days when the only treatment for a struggling newborn was to dangle it by its heels or, in the case of Louis XIII of France, dose it with red wine. Nonetheless, Ms Goldszal worries about the long-term effects of brutal modern procedures, even wondering whether her disabled or confused friends were damaged by them, long before they knew how to complain.

She paints a gripping, vivid picture of life at Necker, especially the nurses, permanently exhausted but perceptive enough to lay a gentle hand on a newborn, turn down the lighting, or rock them. It is the medical setting, among people in scrubs and the tang of sanitiser, that thrills her: she finds herself surrounded at staff meetings by “an entire continent” of words she can’t understand: oesophageal atresia, hypocalcaemia, exomphalos.

Ms Goldszal goes only sketchily into the philosophy of personhood and the difficult legal line between the full-term fetus inside the womb and the newborn outside it. For ideas of ensoulment she has even less time. She envies religious Jews their belief that God breathes life into the fetus at the moment of birth, but she is a modern woman; this is really just “magical thinking”.

All she needs to know she has seen at Necker, and it is “fundamentally prosaic”: new life appears where before there was nothing, and medical ingenuity does its best to ensure that life does not swiftly return to nothing again.

Yet a mystery remains. The last epigram that opens the book comes from J.G. Ballard, an English writer, recording the birth of his daughter: “For the first few seconds I had the sense she was immensely old. She looked like something from Egyptian sculpture, streamlined by time. Within a few seconds she was suddenly very young.” Newborns may seem able to tell us nothing. But perhaps sometimes, for a moment, their eyes and their parents can. ■
