To triumph in show business, take a lo-fi or hi-fi approach

Skydance is stuck in the dangerous middle ground

Nov 12th 2026

TWO TYPES of movie stand out in America’s box-office revival this year. The first is indie fare such as “Obsession” and “Backrooms”, made by two YouTubers who spent peanuts on films that grossed over $920m between them. The second is extravaganzas like “Spider-Man: Brand New Day” and “The Odyssey”, which cost hundreds of millions of dollars and grossed a combined $4.3bn. Such extremes reflect a new topography of Hollywood: the twin peaks of lo-fi creator content and hi-fi premium stuff. As with the rest of show business, anything stuck in the middle is in trouble.

On one extreme sits YouTube, Hollywood’s most potent disruptive force. As we report it has become America’s dominant TV platform, with a share of streaming almost as big as those of Netflix, Skydance and Walt Disney combined. Its business model makes it easy to spend on content, since it pays according to performance rather than buying shows up front, as legacy studios do. Its creators, the best of whom are superstars in their own right, upload an average of 20m videos a day. In an indirect form of flattery, streamers such as Netflix are poaching them.

On the other extreme is content so lavishly produced that watching it is an event in its own right. Take America’s top two blockbusters. They generated about a fifth of all box-office revenues this year; some IMAX tickets for “The Odyssey” sold out a year in advance. Sport and live entertainment also fit the bill. Last year 96 of America’s top 100 telecasts were sports-related.

Lo-fi is a volume play. Hi-fi is a value one. But they have something in common: human connection. YouTube’s creators establish an intimacy with their audiences, born of years of fandom. Driving people to glitzier fare is the thrill of sharing something special, such as the spectacle of the imax or the tension of the Super Bowl. Such experiences need not cost megabucks. A24, an independent studio, has mastered the art of making people not only watch its films but talk about them. Even one of its documentaries—Nathan Fielder’s “You Can See Everything”—is close to blockbuster status.

Each peak is relatively AI-proof. Owned by Google, YouTube offers AI tools to creators. It is the biggest distribution platform for AI-made video content. At the other end, people watch World Cup football matches, and perhaps also films like “The Odyssey”, precisely because they are triumphs of human endeavour. This makes them hard for bots to copy.

Unfortunately most show business lies between Hollywood’s two peaks. For years studio executives have made a virtue of offering all things to all people—with mixed financial results. Skydance, newly created by the merger of Paramount and Warner Bros, two Hollywood studios, is a case in point. Its two streaming platforms, HBO Max and Paramount Plus, cater respectively to film buffs and middle America. Its 50 cable channels range from CNN to the Food Network. Its sports rights straddle American football and tennis. From next year onwards, to satisfy one of the antitrust conditions of the merger, it aims to churn out a new film every fortnight or so. A similar spray-and-pray approach is common across mainstream music, gaming and publishing.

Combine all Skydance’s broadcast, cable and streaming services, and they still draw less attention than YouTube. Bundle together HBO and Paramount Plus, as planned early next year, and they will still be less than half the size of Netflix. As for producing upscale movies, it would be easier if Skydance’s acquisition spree had not saddled it with $87bn of debt.

Netflix may be big enough to survive as a stand-alone streamer. Disney is buoyed by its theme parks. Skydance looks stuck in the mid-size trap. It could join forces with the American arm of TikTok, part-owned by Oracle, to counter YouTube (the chairmen of Oracle and Skydance are father and son). It could use AI to cut operating costs and invest more in premium films and TV. What it cannot do is muddle through. The valley between the peaks is a dangerous place to be. ■
