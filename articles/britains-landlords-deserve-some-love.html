Britain’s landlords deserve some love

Who will speak for the HMO Hero?

Nov 5th 2026

Of all the tax increases Labour have introduced since 2024, one passed with barely a murmur. When John Healey, the chancellor, popped another two percentage points on tax on “income from property” last month, few complained. It was to be expected. Successive governments have hammered landlords, including those who own a “house in multiple occupation” or two, for a decade. Will anyone speak for the hmo Hero?

Not Labour, that is for sure. Mr Healey’s tax rise came after a similar increase the year before. The Renters’ Rights Act, which was passed last year, introduces rent controls in all but name. For just £47 ($63) a tenant can challenge any rent rise, with a tribunal determining whether it is the local market rent. The tribunal can even reduce rents already agreed on. Market prices are, apparently, too important to be left to the market.

Yet when it comes to landlords, Labour are only following in the Tory party’s footsteps. George Osborne, the former Conservative chancellor, removed tax breaks on mortgage interest and replaced them with a less generous tax credit. Landlords were also hit with a 3% stamp-duty surcharge when buying property (which became 5% under Labour). It was under the Tories that the British dream of owning someone else’s home became a nightmare.

Buy-to-let landlords are easy to hate. Partly, it is jealousy. The buy-to-let mortgage was invented in 1996, at the start of a colossal house-price boom, during which real house prices across Britain almost trebled in a decade. It proved a sensational investment: someone who bought in 1996 enjoyed higher returns than most big stock markets.

Partly it is because renting in Britain is often miserable. Britain’s private-rental sector is dominated by amateurs. George Orwell’s caricature of a slum landlord in the 1930s is not far off reality today: “A poor old woman who has invested her life’s savings in three slum houses, inhabits one of them, and tries to live on the rent of the other two—never, in consequence, having any money for repairs.” About half of landlords own just one property. Rather than a well-organised property company, a typical English landlord remains a baby-boomer wondering why their savings product is ringing at 2am to complain about a leak.

At times it seems as if the government sees every private renter as a public-policy failure. What should private renters do if government policy leads to rents going up? Buy a home with the support of the government, comes the reply. The idea that renting is a necessary part of a dynamic economy is alien in a country that treats it like an embarrassing affliction. A world in which everyone owns is one in which people are unable to drop everything and change cities for another job.

Building more homes is the best fix for expensive housing. But a higher proportion of rentals can still help. A terrace in a city can house either a small family or half a dozen young renters, if broken into flats or turned into an hmo. Landlords are often painted as parasites. This is unfair. They are worms, playing a vital part in the housing food chain, digesting what others would waste.

Hammering them may backfire, eventually. The Office for Budget Responsibility has warned that potential landlords may steer clear, reducing rental supply and pushing up rents. Housing supply is a fractious political debate in Britain; landlord supply is its forgotten cousin. What reason do landlords have to stay? Spectacular returns are long gone. At 5.8%, the typical gross yield on a buy-to-let property is barely higher than the yield on a ten-year gilt, the closest thing to risk-free return. Buy-to-let offers return-free risk. Who is likelier to default: the government or a tenant?

And yet economic logic goes out the window when it comes to Britons and property. The government is harder on landlords than it should be; likewise landlords appear to be suckers for punishment. It would be economically rational for more to leave, yet surprisingly few do. Well-reasoned predictions of a mass landlord exodus and rental supply crunch have been made repeatedly in the past decade. Neither has come to pass. Indeed, the stock of rental homes has grown by about 1% a year since 2021, says the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, a leftie think-tank. Chris Norris of the National Residential Landlords Association puts it delicately: landlords are “not entirely rational actors”.

Would more rational actors be welcome? British governments of various stripes have long called for institutional investors to replace Orwell’s grannies. Renters should be careful what they wish for. Lazy landlords are a blessing and a curse. A Boomer landlord who would rather not worry on their cruise might not raise a good tenant’s monthly rent; a corporation will do what the spreadsheet says. Whether smart money wants to invest in a sector with low returns and huge regulatory risk is another matter.

Perhaps only Britain’s crack squad of hmo Heroes are foolhardy enough for the task. Come 2030, landlords will have to climate-proof their properties at a cost of up to £10,000. And any landlord who banks on strong house-price growth may end up disappointed. The government has not yet equalised the rates of capital-gains tax and income tax, but rumours that it will do so persist.

These risks are known, yet still the hmo Heroes march towards gunfire. Banks have seen no drop-off in the number of people taking out new buy-to-let mortgages. It is brave to the point of foolishness. At some point it becomes a form of intergenerational altruism, with wealthy boomers investing in a buy-to-let when doing almost anything else with their money would leave them better off. Still it is a lousy financial decision that someone else can live in. Why try to stop them? Britain may not like its landlords, but it would miss them if they were gone.■
